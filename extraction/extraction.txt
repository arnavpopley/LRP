OBJECTIVE 1: chemistry + H_2 budget

Ehhalt & Rohrer 2009 (Tellus B 61, 500–535) [journal's open-access PDF]
• Sources, Tg/yr (Table 1 p.507; abstract p.500): fossil fuel 11 ± 4; biomass burning 15 ± 6; N_2 fixation ocean 6 ± 3, land 3 ± 2; from CH_4 23 ± 8; from other VOC 18 ± 7 —> 76 ± 14
• Sinks: OH 19 ± 5; soil 60 (+30/−20) —> 79; burden 155 ± 10 Tg; lifetime 2.0yr
• ~50% of production is anthropogenic (p.529)
• Soil sink sits in two clusters the authors call "clearly not compatible" (p.522): ~55 Tg/yr bottom-up (Hauglustaine & Ehhalt 55; Price 55; Sanderson 58.3) vs ~86 top-down (Rhee 88 ± 11 from isotopes; Xiao 84 ± 8 from inversion) (pp.521–522)
• They pick 60 and stretch the upper error to reach the high cluster (p.522) —> this is where +30/−20 comes from
• Why they favour the low cluster: a high soil sink needs more H_2 from VOC, which breaks the CO budget (p.500, p.522)
• Deposition velocity 0.01–0.1 cm/s across biomes; uptake peaks near 10% soil moisture and is zero when dry or above 23–30%; top few cm only; snow and wet pores block diffusion (p.518)
• OH sink: k = 2.8 × 10^−12 exp(−1800/T) cm^3/s; the ±5 comes mainly from the CH_3CCl_3 lifetime (p.517)
• Mixing ratio 531 ± 6 ppb (1991–96) (p.501); NH lower by ~3% (p.503) —> ~70% of soil uptake is in the NH (p.528) even though ~64% of sources are too (p.529)
• No long-term trend (p.502)
• Hydrogen economy (Sect 6 p.530): early studies assumed 3–20% leakage —> burden ×1.3 (Schultz; "more realistic") to ×4 (Tromp); Warwick 2004 finds a small stratospheric impact; no GWP and no climate statement
• Open needs: year-long soil-uptake field studies across ecosystems; H_2 production from VOC (p.530)

OBJECTIVE 2: how GWP is estimated

Chen, Solomon & Stone 2024 (Front Energy Res 12:1463450)
• Model: 0D photochemical box model, 66 reactions (Sect 2.2 p.3); pulse of 53 ppb H_2, ~10% of burden (p.3, p.6)
• GWP_100 10 (+7/−4); GWP_20 28 (+18/−11) (Table 5 p.8)
• GWP_100 by component: CH_4 4.4; trop O_3 2.4; strat H_2O 2.7 (Table 5 p.8)
• Soil loss rate 0.4/yr; soil lifetime range 1.7–2.9yr (p.2; Table 1 p.3); overall H_2 lifetime: not found
• CH_4 feedback factor: 1.4 full mechanism; 1.7 four-equation system (Sect 3.1 p.6)
• Four-equation system overstates peak CH_4 response by ~85%; time-integrated by 123% (p.6)
• Stated limit: no spatial resolution —> cannot say whether the global GWP depends on where soil loss happens (p.9)


OBJECTIVE 3: estimates compared + spread over time

Warwick et al 2023 (ACP 23, 13451–13467)
• Model: UKESM1; 40yr steady-state runs, last 25 analysed (p.13454); H_2 and CH_4 as fixed lower boundary conditions (p.13452); H_2 at 500 (base), 750, 1000, 1500, 2000 ppb (Table 1 p.13454)
• Method: extends Fuglestvedt 2010; Eqs 1–5 (pp.13459–13460)
• GWP_100 12 ± 6 in text (p.13451, p.13462); Table 3 total 11.5 (6–18) (p.13461)
• GWP_20 34.8 (19–51) AR5 basis; 34.4 (19–51) AR6 basis (Table 3 p.13461)
• Components (AR6 basis): CH_4 5.2; trop O_3 3.2; strat H_2O 3.1 (Table 3) —> ~2/3 troposphere, 1/3 stratosphere (p.13462)
• H_2 lifetime 1.96yr (1.4–2.2) (p.13460); soil uptake scales with H_2 (constant deposition velocity) (p.13453)
• CH_4 feedback factor 1.39; perturbation lifetime 11.8yr (pp.13460–13461)
• Uncertainty covers: soil-uptake lifetime; forcing scaling factors; AGWP_CO2. Excludes: chemical lifetimes; stratospheric response; aerosol and cloud responses (pp.13462–13463)
• Leakage: 1% and 10% —> ~9 and ~96 Tg H_2/yr (p.13453) —> offsets ~0.4% and ~4% of ~27,200 Tg CO_2e/yr saved (p.13463)
• Caveat they state: model ERF sensitivity to CH_4 ~65% larger than the scaling factors imply (p.13463) —> GWP uses the smaller figure

Bryant et al 2024 (Front Energy Res 12:1415593)
• Model: UKESM1 atmosphere-only v11.1 (p.2); +10% surface H_2 as a lower boundary condition; separate +10% CH_4 run scaled via Eq 1 (pp.3–4); 15yr spin-up, final year analysed (p.4)
• Tropospheric GWP_100: 9.4 at present NO_x (CH_4 5.5 + O_3 3.9); 9.1 at 30% NO_x (CH_4 5.5 + O_3 3.5) (Tables 2–3 p.11)
• Deposition 44–73 Tg/yr —> 7.8–11.2 (p.11); base deposition 57 Tg/yr (pp.9–10)
• Stratospheric component not estimated (p.11); quotes Warwick 2023 tropospheric 8.4 of 11.5 total
• Conclusion: current GWP "can therefore be applied to future scenarios of differing NO_x"; VOC and CO not varied (p.12)
• H_2 lifetime and feedback factor: not found in text

Skeie et al 2025 (ACP 25, 4929–4942)
• Model: OsloCTM3; emissions-driven for H_2; CH_4 concentration-driven (p.4931) —> Sand was mostly concentration-driven (p.4934)
• Leak size 0.1–100 Tg/yr —> 12.2–12.6 (Sect 3.1 p.4933)
• Location (1 Tg/yr point sources; Table 1 p.4933): South Pacific 14.2; Antarctica 14.2; Arctic 11.7; 4 continental sites 10.2–11.9 —> 19% below to 14% above the base 12.5
• 2050 backgrounds: 12.9 (SSP119) to 13.5 (SSP585) (p.4936)
• H_2 lifetime 2.35yr total; 7.02yr atmospheric; 3.53yr soil (Table 3 p.4935); perturbation lifetime 1.77–2.59yr across sites (p.4938)
• CH_4 feedback factor 1.46 now —> 1.71 in SSP434 (Table 5 p.4937); CH_4 GWP_100 27.0 —> 28.6–30.5 (p.4937)
• Conclusion: GWP higher further from soil-sink regions (pp.4938–4939); regional GWP reporting "not necessary for hydrogen" (p.4939)
• Limits they state: soil sink = largest uncertainty; model CH_4 lifetime 6.85yr vs assessed 9.1 ± 0.9 (p.4938)

Bertagni et al 2022 (Nat Commun 13:7706) [also objective 4c]
• Model: 4-species steady-state box model (CH_4, H_2, CO, OH) + lumped OH sink (p.6); extends Prather's CH_4–CO–OH system
• Parameters: H_2 lifetime ~2yr; soil uptake 75% of removal (k_d = 0.38/yr); OH split 45% X, 36% CO, 14% CH_4, 5% H_2 (Table 1 p.6); feedback factor ~1.5 (p.7)
• Own GWP_100 15.6; GWP_20 44 (CH_4-mediated part 7.8 and 22) (Methods p.7) —> "upper range" of published values; compares with Warwick 11 ± 5 and 33 (+11/−13)
• Critical H_2 loss: green ~9% (9 ± 3% in Discussion p.5; 8–10% in Results p.4); blue 7% at 0.2% CH_4 leak; ~4.5% at 0.5%; ~zero at 1% (p.4)
• Worst case (blue, 2% CH_4 loss): up to 22% of climate benefit offset over 100yr; 65% over 20yr (p.5)
• Soil sink ±20% shifts the threshold (Fig 4 p.5); no number given
• Limits they state: no 3D transport; O_3 and strat H_2O enter only via the radiative estimate, not the box model (p.5, p.7)

Derwent 2018 (BEIS Research Paper No. 21; dated 17 Oct 2018, cover Nov 2018)
• GWP_100 4.3 (95% range 0–9.8) (p.3, p.21)
• Derivation: starts from Derwent 2001's 5.8 —> adjusted for OH/soil sink split (7.8; 2.1–13.5) —> O_3-to-CH_4 slope (3.1; 0–6.2) —> IPCC 2013 forcing (4.3; 0–9.8) (p.14, pp.20–21)
• Admits it rests on one model and one older study (p.21)
• 1% leak realistic for UK domestic use (pp.3–4, p.23) —> cuts 54.0 Mt CO_2/yr saving to 53.7 (0.6%) (p.23)
• Notes Warwick 2004 used a 5% leak scenario (p.11)


OBJECTIVE 4a: how much leaks + infrastructure

Frazer-Nash for BEIS 2022
• Method: mechanisms per element from industry input + natural-gas data; Bayesian network, not Monte Carlo (p.39); 50% = median, 99% = "credible maximum" (p.11, p.39)
• Total: 0.96% (50%); 1.50% (99%) of 476 TWh central scenario (p.8)
• By stage (50% / 99%) (p.6; Table 4 pp.39–40): electrolysis with venting 3.32 / 9.20; electrolysis with recombination 0.24 / 0.52; CCUS-enabled 0.25 / 0.50 (assumed); transmission 0.04 / 0.48; distribution 0.26 / 0.53; salt cavern 0.02 / 0.06; above-ground storage 2.77 / 6.52; gas trailers 0.30 / 0.66; liquid trailers 3.76 / 13.20; residential 0.30 / 0.69; refuelling 0.25 / 0.89; fuel cells with purging 1.36 / 2.64
• Distribution network = largest single emitter by volume (p.8, p.42)
• H_2 vs CH_4 through the same leak (p.24): laminar volumetric ~1.2, mass ~0.15; turbulent volumetric ~2.8, mass ~0.35; H21 tests measured 1.1 and 2.8 (p.27)
• Porosity: >90% polyethylene by 2032 (p.26); permeation through pipe walls NOT included —> H21 tests did not consider "long term, slow permeation of gas through the asset materials" (p.28); flagged for further work (p.48)
• Homes: boiler start-up/shutdown 0.13%; pipework ~0.32%, "likely to be a considerable overestimate" (p.36)
• Odorant, sensors, leak detection: not found
• Assumed, not calculated: CCUS production 0–0.5% (p.22); process industry 0–0.5% (p.38); electrolysis purge upper bound 10% (p.19)

Esquivel-Elizondo et al 2023 (Front Energy Res 11:1207208)
• Whole chain 0.2–20% (pp.4–5); climate studies usually assume 1–10% (p.5)
• By component (p.4): electrolysis 0.03–9.2%; blue (SMR + CCS) 0.0–1.5%; grey 0.5–1.0%; liquefaction 0.15–10.0%; transmission pipes 0.02–5.0%; distribution pipes 0.0003–5.0%; above-ground storage 2.8–6.5%; gas trucks 0.3–2.3%; liquid trucks 2.0–13.2%; liquid handling 2.0–20%; gas refuelling 0.25–3.0%; liquid refuelling 2.0–15.0%; power generation 0.01–3.0%
• Basis: 5 core studies (van Ruijven 2011; Arrigoni 2022; Cooper 2022; Frazer-Nash 2022; Fan 2022) (p.2); all from assumption, natural-gas proxy, lab work, or simulation (pp.3–4)
• No direct measurements (p.2); commercial sensors read ppm —> site quantification needs ~10 ppb and a response of seconds (p.2, p.5)
• Widest uncertainty: everything liquid; electrolysis (p.4)

Westra et al 2024 (Sci Rep 14:24147)
• Method: AirCore sampler on a car and a drone + gas chromatography; precision < 2 ppb (pp.2–3)
• Site: Chemical Park Delfzijl (~4 km^2): chlor-alkali electrolysis (surplus H_2 vented); H_2O_2 plant with SMR; fuelling station with storage + compressor (p.5)
• Emission rates: 0.5–2.1 g/s (mass balance); 0.1–2.0 g/s (Gaussian plume); mass balance 2.3× higher on average (p.11)
• Loss up to 4.2% "relative to estimated daily production" (p.11); no single uncertainty given
• Sources: "leakage, purging and storage" (p.11); no split between them
• Sample: 7 car transects; 4 drone flights; 3 good downwind transects (p.6); park "not fully representative" of the H_2 value chain (p.5)
• States that modelled 1–10% loss estimates have "not been validated at all by actual measurements" (p.2)

Arrigoni & Bravo Diaz 2022 (JRC130362)
• Industry figures, mostly Air Liquide (pp.15–17): electrolysis ~0.2% now —> 0.03% in 2030; SMR 0.0001% (flared); tube trailers < 1%; liquid delivery ~10%; refuelling 3–8.5%; compressed chain to station ~4.2%; liquid chain up to 20%; pipelines ~1% —> < 0.7% by 2030
• 2050: losses 13.3 Mt ≈ 2% of production; 54% production, 22% delivery, 24% end use (Table 3 p.17)
• Repurposed gas pipes: H_2 fugitive rate 1.25–3× the CH_4 rate (SNAM citing Kiwa 2019; p.17)
• Sensors built for 1,000–10,000 ppm (safety); emissions work needs "five orders of magnitude greater" sensitivity (p.38); most sensors cannot go below 100 ppm (p.28)
• H_2 has no infrared absorption feature —> CH_4 optical tools do not transfer; only Raman is specific and quantitative (p.26, p.37)
• Conclusion: 1–5% loss expected, ~2% by 2050 (p.37); renewable H_2 still a large net benefit (p.2)
• Odorants: not found

Cooper et al 2022 (Sci Total Environ 830:154624) [Imperial's open repository copy; published version]
• Method: emissions = throughput × emission factor per stage; downstream rates from natural-gas data adjusted for H_2 properties; "no H_2 measurement studies" exist (pp.4–5); 20,000 Monte Carlo runs (pp.5–6)
• Whole supply chain: green 2.6% (0.1–6.9%); blue and biomass 1.4% (0.1–4.3%) (Sect 3.1.1 p.6; Fig 3 p.7)
• By stage (Table 2 p.6): production blue 0.55% (0.10–1.00%), green 2.05% (0.10–4.00%); compression ~0.18%; liquefaction ~0.33% (up to 2.2%); transmission + storage 0.03% (0.02–0.05%); distribution 0.08% (0.05–0.16%)
• End use excluded (p.6)
• H_2 emission rates "comparable" to CH_4 rates from gas supply; warming impact "29 (0.6 to 66) times lower" (p.10)
• GWP: cites 1.9–9.8 from Derwent 2006/2018/2020 (p.2); GWP_100 only; exact value used not stated in the main text
• Limits they state: flaring and maintenance excluded —> may underestimate; unknown whether H_2 leaks at the CH_4 rate; most source data rated low quality (Table 3 p.9)

OBJECTIVE 4b: detection + odorisation

Environment Agency / NPL 2025 (SC230001/R)
• "no established methods" for monitoring H_2 emissions to atmosphere (p.6); "There are currently no standard methods for monitoring hydrogen." (p.62)
• Instruments give concentration; an emission rate also needs flow, a sampling strategy, and validation (p.7, p.46); "no existing correlation curves" for H_2 (p.54)
• 7 instruments tested (pp.36–37); 4 reach roughly ppm; the rest ~1% v/v (p.42); humidity dominates below 5 ppm (p.42)
• No commercial remote-sensing system for H_2 (p.35)
• Expects venting from electrolysis to dominate; fugitive pipework emissions "relatively small" if purpose-built (p.56) —> an assumption, not a measurement
• Snapshot surveys: "non-continuous leaks may be missed" (p.58)
• Next steps: an H_2 version of the high-flow sampler; field validation against a controlled release (p.62)
• Odorants: not mentioned


Mouli-Castillo et al 2021, "A comparative study of odorants for gas escape detection of natural gas and hydrogen" (IJHE 46(27), 14881–14893; 10.1016/j.ijhydene.2021.01.211) [NEW source; open repository copy without page numbers]
• 3 odorants, all sulphur-based (Table 1): NB (78% tert-butylthiol + 22% dimethyl sulfide); Standby 2 (NB in hexane); THT
• 18 tests in a 54 m^3 room; 3–4 untrained people per test; 0–5 smell scale (Methods)
• H_2 + NB smelled at 500, 1,000, and 10,000 ppm gas-in-air; methane + NB only at 10,000 ppm (Results; Table 2) —> authors cannot say why
• THT in H_2: smelled at 10,000 ppm but not recognised as a gas leak (Discussion)
• Sulphur odorants "toxic to fuel cells" (Introduction); pipe-material compatibility not tested
• Smell works from ~500 ppm of gas in air upward —> a safety threshold; nothing here on small chronic leaks (the second half is my inference, not theirs)

• Lifetime 2.8yr from their two-box optimisation (p.617) —> longer than the ~2–2.5yr everyone else uses
• Leakage assumed 1 ± 0.5% of industrial H_2 production —> 0.7 Tg/yr; "relatively unconstrained because of a lack of measurements" (p.620)
• Trends 1990–2020: CH_4 oxidation the largest growing source (+~4 Tg in total); production leakage +0.015 Tg/yr/yr; soil and OH sinks both rising (p.617; Fig 1 p.618)
• Surface H_2 523.4 ppb (1992) —> 543.5 ppb (2020); ~555 ppb in 2024 (p.617)
• Uses GWP_100 11 ± 4 and GWP_20 37 ± 18 from earlier studies (p.616–617) —> no new GWP
• Temperature: H_2 change contributed 0.020 ± 0.006 °C (abstract; p.621; see conflict 13); future SSP range 0.01–0.05 °C depending on use, leakage, and CH_4 (p.621)
• Low-warming SSPs: cutting CH_4 cuts H_2 made from CH_4 —> H_2 warming can fall below today's at 1% leakage but rises at 10% (p.621; Fig 5 p.623)
• Soil-sink research need: maximum biological uptake rate and soil properties (pp.621–622)

OBJECTIVE 2

Yang et al 2025 (GRL 52, e2024GL112445) [file]
• Model: GEOS-Chem v14.3.0, 4° × 5°, 2016 meteorology; H_2 and CH_4 as fixed surface concentrations; +10% perturbations; 3yr spin-up, 4th year reported (pp.2–3); follows Sand's GWP framework; strat H_2O taken from Sand, not computed (p.3)
• Problem: models underestimate OH reactivity (by 30% in remote air up to ×2–10 at continental surfaces) —> too much OH —> CH_4 and O_3 too sensitive to H_2 (p.2)
• Fix: added missing VOC emissions + a terminal OH sink over continents (p.3) —> they say it has no mechanistic basis
• CH_4 lifetime against tropospheric OH: 8.5yr standard —> 11.4yr modified vs 11.2 ± 1.3 observed (p.3)
• H_2 OH sink 24 —> 18 Tg/yr; H_2 lifetime 2.3 —> 2.5yr (soil fixed at 59 Tg/yr) (p.3)
• GWP_100: Sand ensemble recomputed 11.4 ± 1.9 (soil uncertainty excluded); standard GEOS-Chem 10.8; modified 8.8 = 20% lower (p.5; Fig 2 p.6)
• Of the drop: CH_4 contribution −17%; O_3 contribution −31% (p.7)
• Table 1 (p.5): CH_4 lifetime and feedback in every model used for H_2 GWP: ensemble 8.3 ± 0.9yr, f 1.45 ± 0.07; GFDL 9.5yr, 1.40; UKESM1 (Warwick 2023) 8.5yr, 1.39; IPCC AR5 11.2 ± 1.3yr, 1.34 ± 0.06; AR6 f 1.30 ± 0.07
• Their own caveat: 20% drop still within current uncertainty, but moves the best estimate; soil sink remains the dominant uncertainty (p.7)
• Fixed-concentration runs miss OH feedback on H_2 production: Sand's GFDL and OsloCTM gave 12.8 emission-driven vs 11.4 and 13.0 concentration-driven (p.7)

OBJECTIVE 4a: porosity

Shin et al 2026 (IJHE 239, 155404) [file]
• Measured H_2 permeation through MDPE and HDPE: lab desorption (TDA) at 100–2,500 psi + direct pipe tests at 10 bar (pp.2–5)
• Loss through the pipe wall: < 10.5 g/km/day at 2–10 bar, room temperature (abstract; Conclusion p.8); 6" SDR11 pipe 7.0–9.5 g/km/day measured, 7.3–10.5 by TDA; ½" SDR9.3 6.0–8.7 (p.8)

OBJECTIVE 4b: odorants

Mouli-Castillo et al 2020 (IJHE 45, 11875–11884) [file]
• 5 odorants (Table 1 p.11877): NB (78% tert-butyl mercaptan + 22% dimethyl sulphide; SGN's primary); Standby 2 (NB in hexane); THT; GASODOR S-Free (sulphur-free, acrylates; used in Germany); 5-ethylidene-2-norbornene (sulphur-free, fuel-cell compatible)
• Method: accredited olfactometry to EN 13725; 6 trained panellists; detection threshold, intensity, hedonic tone, character (pp.11877–11878)
• Result: 4 pass as stenching agents; 5-ethylidene-2-norbornene fails —> perceived as pleasant at every concentration (abstract; p.11879)
• Range of the passing odorants: 20,789–148,361 odour units/m^3 (p.11879)
• Context they give: sulphur compounds are toxic to fuel cells and no odorant in use today is fuel-cell compatible (p.11876); hydrogen LFL 4% by volume (p.11876)
• Key engineering point: the one fuel-cell-compatible odorant tested is the one that failed —> odorising H_2 for safety conflicts with using it in fuel cells
